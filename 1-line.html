THE FAIRYTALE
When you scroll through social media, moving abroad often looks easy. However, packing your life into a suitcase means stepping out of your comfort zone.
Suddenly, you start missing the little things you took for granted. While the professional rewards and personal growth can be rewarding, your journey demands immense resilience.

"Isolation, bullying, language and cultural barriers, different schedules, different schools, and racism are among the small challenges of daily life. The major ones were when relatives started passing away. When you wish you could be there, but you have to be here. A person living abroad is and always will be torn in two. Last year, my brother was in the hospital for six months, enduring a very difficult open-heart surgery and a post-operative coma. I had to stay here for work, but also be there to help, constantly going back and forth, which was costly both financially and emotionally. Always divided. I am here and I miss Italy; I am there and I want to return to the Netherlands".

Luisa. 65 years old.
From Rome to Dublin and then Amsterdam.

If you decide to pack your bags, you need to understand that the Italian brain drain is not a temporary trend, it has become a structural shift.

First phase


Preceding the global financial crisis and the dawn of the Great Recession in 2008, this phase is defined by relatively modest and well-balanced mobility. Outward migration steadily averages 40,000 individuals per year, whereas return migration stands at 33,000

14

Second phase


Outward migration experiences a sharp upsurge (from 50,000 to 89,000), while return migration persists at considerably more modest levels (averaging 30,000 annually). The interplay of these two trends drives a decline in net migration, hitting a trough of -60,000 in 2014 and culminating in an aggregate loss of 170,000 Italian citizens over the period as a whole.

19

Third phase


Outward migration reaches unprecedented levels (averaging 114,000 annually), yet the influx of returning Italians also increases (to 45,000 per year). This dynamic facilitates a relative contraction in the net migration gap, shifting from -72,000 in 2015 to -54,000 in 2019

23

Fourth phase


Pandemic shock. Outward migration sustains elevated levels, compounded by the Brexit effect: numerous Italians already domiciled in the United Kingdom expedited their enrollment in the AIRE to secure or validate their settled status, thereby inflating emigration records. Return migration also experiences an uptick (205,000 in aggregate), prompted by public health uncertainty, culminating in a net migration deficit of 110,000 for the entire three-year timeframe.

24

Now


In 2024 alone, over 150,000 Italians officially moved their residency abroad. Today, the "Italy outside of Italy" counts over 6.4 million people.


Historically, if you were part of this exodus, you were almost exclusively from the South, driven by a sheer lack of local opportunities. Today, the landscape has changed. Europe remains the ultimate comfort zone, welcoming over half of all Italian expats.
The real motivation? It’s not that you don’t want to live in Italy. It’s that as a young professional, you are looking for meritocracy, transparent career paths, and contracts that actually allow you to become independent and achieve your goals.

"In Italy, right after graduating in biology, job opportunities were extremely limited, primarily geared toward teaching. Ever since I was a little girl, I had dreamed of doing research.
I found an innovative university to do my PhD, with young and motivated professors. I found the possibility, post-PhD, to receive offers based on meritocracy and not on the development of good "political" connections."

Anna. 30 years old.
From Milan to Boston.
